Concept 1 · Ages 8–18

🤖 Build Your Own AI Helper

From "I use AI" to "I designed one."

Student outcome

Each student picks a real person and a real problem: a study buddy for a younger sibling, a recipe helper for a grandparent, a practice coach for their basketball team. They design an AI assistant for that person by choosing its name, personality, what it's good at, its rules, and what it should refuse to do. By the end of the session they have a working helper, tested with real questions, and a one-page "helper blueprint" that explains their design choices.

Why families would love it

  • Parents hear about AI every day and wonder how their child should use it. Here the child learns to build it responsibly, not just consume it.
  • The helper is made for someone at home, so the family is part of the project. Grandma actually gets to try her recipe helper.
  • Safety is part of the design, not a lecture. Students write rules like "never ask for personal information" and "give hints, don't do the homework," then check that their helper follows them.

How it grows into a deeper project

  1. Design and test: build the first helper and try it with three real questions.
  2. Give it knowledge: the student writes a fact sheet (team plays, family recipes, study notes) and makes the helper answer only from it.
  3. Measure it: run a test sheet, score the answers, find where it gets things wrong, and fix it. This is how real AI teams work.
  4. Give it a face: design the app screen people would use and publish it on the student's portfolio.
  5. Pitch it: present the helper to a visiting mentor as a product: who it's for, the problem, and the evidence that it works.

Students' own ideas and decisions

Every choice belongs to the student: who it's for, what problem matters, how it should talk, and where the line is on what it won't do. They also decide what "good" means, by writing the test questions and judging the answers themselves. When the helper gets something wrong, they choose the fix. These are product decisions, and they are the core skill.

Concept 2 · Ages 8–18

📷 Invent a Smart Gadget

Teach a computer to see, then invent something with it.

Student outcome

Students train their own image, sound, or pose model with Google's Teachable Machine, a free browser tool with no accounts or code needed. Then they turn it into an invention: a recycling sorter that tells paper from plastic, a posture coach that notices slouching, a reader for sign-language letters, a pet-door helper that recognises their dog. They leave with a working model that runs on a web page in their portfolio, on any laptop with a camera.

Why families would love it

  • It feels like magic within minutes: the computer starts recognising what the student shows it. Children want to demonstrate it at home that evening.
  • It works from age 8 with no accounts, which makes it easy to say yes to.
  • It leads straight into science fairs, robotics clubs, and hardware projects, which families already value.

How it grows into a deeper project

  1. Train it: teach the model three things to tell apart, with examples the student collects.
  2. Break it: let friends try it and find where it fails, for example in different lighting, with different people, or with new objects. This leads into data and fairness: who did we forget to include?
  3. Improve it: collect better examples, retrain, and compare results before and after.
  4. Build the gadget: put the model in a web page, or connect it to a micro:bit so it lights up, beeps, or moves.
  5. Pitch it: present the invention to a mentor, or enter it in a science fair.

Students' own ideas and decisions

Students choose the problem their gadget solves, what it should recognise, and which examples to teach it. When it fails, they decide whether the fix is more data, different data, or a different design. Older students also decide when the gadget is "good enough" to use, and how to explain its limits honestly.

How each concept adapts by age

Every age group makes the same kind of thing. What changes is who does the technical steps and how deep the thinking goes. GitHub and AI accounts are only for students aged 13 and over, with parental consent.

Age🤖 AI Helper📷 Smart Gadget
8–10 Design the helper on a paper card. The instructor runs it on the projector, and the class tests it together. Train a model together in small groups (for example, happy and sad faces, or rock, paper, scissors) and invent a use for it.
11–12 Use the Helper Builder to make every design choice. The instructor runs their helper live and they score the answers. Train their own model, test it with classmates, and improve it.
13–18 The full product cycle: user interview, design, test sheet, knowledge file, and a pitch. A full invention with a fairness test, before-and-after results, and an optional micro:bit build.

Where the portfolio fits

The portfolio isn't the headline. It's the trophy shelf. Each helper and gadget gets a project card on the student's own website, with what they built, who it's for, and what they learned. Over a term it becomes evidence of real work for university and program applications, written up the way admissions essays and interviews ask for.

The first-session plan and the sample student page already exist, so the portfolio can be set up in any session where students have something to put on it.

Why these two, and how I'd run them

I build AI products for a living. At Xappert, our product work starts from what users need and then tests whether the solution actually works for them. These concepts give students that same process at their level: find a real person, solve a real problem, test it, improve it, and explain it. It's the bridge between learning about AI and the entrepreneurial thinking TRNT wants students to develop.

Safety by design: students use first names only, and no personal information goes into any AI tool. Students under 13 never need an account, because the instructor runs the AI. AI helps students build and test their projects, and public pages never hold passwords or API keys.
